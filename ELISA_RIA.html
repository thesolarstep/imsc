Immunoassay Basics

Welcome to immunoassays. Before diving into laboratory instrumentation, we first establish the foundational rules of biological recognition that make these assays work.

Core Definitions

  • What is an antigen? Any foreign molecule, protein, pathogen component, or small chemical that can be specifically bound by an antibody.
  • What is an antibody? A Y-shaped protective immunoglobulin protein produced by B-cells that binds tightly and selectively to a designated antigen target.
  • What is an epitope? The specific localized surface region on the antigen recognized directly by the antibody's antigen-binding site.
  • What is antigen-antibody specificity? The precise chemical and structural "lock-and-key" fit between antibody paratopes and antigen epitopes, mediated by non-covalent hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and hydrophobic interactions.
  • What is an immunoassay? A biochemical analytical test that uses the specific binding of an antibody to its target antigen to detect or quantify a substance in a sample.

Why Binding Enables Detection

An antibody acts as a molecular "bloodhound." Because it binds only to its corresponding target even in complex biological mixtures (such as blood serum, urine, or cell lysates), we can harness this binding to generate a measurable optical or radioactive signal:

Target Molecule (Analyte) → Specific Antibody Recognition → Measurable Signal (Enzyme / Radioisotope) → Infer Presence or Exact Concentration

Qualitative vs Quantitative Detection

Qualitative Detection (Yes / No)

Determines whether the target is present or absent above a threshold cutoff (e.g., rapid pregnancy tests or initial virus screening).

Quantitative Detection (How Much)

Measures the exact numerical concentration (e.g., pg/mL or ng/mL) by comparing sample signal to a calibrated standard curve.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

2.1 What is ELISA?

ELISA stands for Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay. In simple terms: an antibody or antigen is immobilized onto a solid surface, specific molecular binding occurs, and an enzyme-conjugated reporter antibody converts a colorless substrate into a colored product whose absorbance reveals whether (and how much) target is present.

Solid Surface
Polystyrene 96-well microplate with hydrophobic binding sites
Target Analyte
Antigen or antibody in serum, saliva, urine, or culture media
Enzyme-Linked Component
Reporter antibody conjugated to an enzyme (HRP or AP)
Signal Readout
Chromogenic color change measured as optical density (absorbance)

2.2 Basic ELISA Workflow

Every ELISA format follows a consistent step-by-step logic:

1. Coat Solid Well → 2. Add Sample → 3. Ag-Ab Binding → 4. Wash Well → 5. Add Enzyme-Linked Ab → 6. Wash Well → 7. Add Substrate → 8. Measure Absorbance
Crucial Concept — Why is washing essential?
Washing removes all unbound antigens, unadsorbed antibodies, and free enzyme conjugates. If unbound enzyme-linked antibodies are not completely washed away, they will react with the added substrate and produce intense false-positive background color, ruining the assay!

2.3 The Four Major ELISA Formats

ELISA assays are configured in four primary formats, depending on whether we directly label the primary antibody, use a secondary antibody, sandwich the target between two antibodies, or compete with a known reference.

Format What It Detects Core Principle Minimal Workflow Signal Origin Common Use
Direct ELISA Antigen in sample Enzyme is directly chemically attached to the primary antibody. Ag coated → labelled 1° Ab → wash → substrate Enzyme on primary antibody Fast screening, simple antigen titration
Indirect ELISA Specific antibody in serum Unlabelled primary antibody is bound by an enzyme-linked secondary antibody. Ag coated → 1° Ab → wash → labelled 2° Ab → wash → substrate Enzyme on secondary antibody (amplified signal) Antibody screening (e.g., anti-HIV or viral titer tests)
Sandwich ELISA Antigen (with ≥2 epitopes) Antigen is sandwiched between a capture antibody and a detection antibody. Capture Ab → sample Ag → detection Ab → enzyme-label → substrate Enzyme on detection complex Cytokines, hormones, complex sample matrices (high specificity)
Competitive ELISA Small antigens / haptens Sample antigen competes with labelled reference antigen for limited antibody. Sample Ag + labelled Ag + Ab → wash → substrate Enzyme on bound reference antigen (inversely related!) Drug testing, toxin analysis, low-molecular-weight metabolites

Explore Each Format In Detail

What It Detects
Antigen directly coated to the well
Basic Principle
The primary antibody itself carries the enzyme label. Only one antibody is required.
Minimal Workflow
Target antigen coated → wash → enzyme-labelled 1° Ab → wash → substrate → signal
What Produces Signal?
Enzyme conjugated directly onto the primary antibody

Target antibody in a biological sample (e.g., patient serum)

Why use a secondary antibody?
1) Signal amplification (multiple secondary antibodies bind one primary antibody); 2) Flexibility (one labelled secondary antibody works for many assays).

Antigen in crude/complex samples (e.g., blood, plasma)

The antigen is caught and held between two specific antibodies: a bottom capture antibody and a top detection antibody.

Because the antigen is firmly packed between two antibody "slices of bread"! Requires the antigen to have at least two distinct epitopes.

ELISA Signal & Interpretation

Once incubation and washes are complete, how does molecular binding turn into an actual number on your lab notebook?

The Enzymatic Chromogenic Reaction

ELISA relies on an enzyme converting a colorless substrate into a colored product:

Enzyme Conjugate + Colorless Substrate → Colored Product → Optical Absorbance (OD) Read at Specific λ
1. Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)

Very common small enzyme. Commonly paired with substrate TMB (tetramethylbenzidine), producing a vivid blue color that turns yellow upon adding sulfuric acid stop solution (read at 450 nm).

2. Alkaline Phosphatase (AP)

Common alternative enzyme. Commonly paired with substrate pNPP (p-nitrophenyl phosphate), which yields a soluble bright yellow product (read at 405 nm).

Essential Assay Controls

Raw absorbance numbers are meaningless without proper internal controls run side-by-side in the same plate:

  • Blank Well: Contains buffer and substrate, but no antibody or analyte. Used to subtract baseline instrument optical absorbance.
  • Positive Control: Contains a known standard of the target. Confirms that all reagents, enzymes, and detection steps functioned properly.
  • Negative Control: Contains sample matrix without target. Confirms that there is no non-specific background binding or false-positive signal.
  • Standards: A series of known target concentrations used to generate the reference curve.
  • Unknown Sample: The clinical or experimental specimen whose target concentration is to be calculated.

The Standard Curve Concept

To quantify an unknown sample, we plot a standard curve: known standard concentrations on the X-axis versus measured absorbance on the Y-axis. By measuring the absorbance of the unknown sample, we can interpolate its exact concentration.

Remember the Direction of the Relationship!
  • Direct, Indirect, and Sandwich ELISA: Higher target concentration → MORE enzyme bound → HIGHER absorbance (Direct relationship).
  • Competitive ELISA: Higher target concentration → LESS enzyme bound → LOWER absorbance (Inverse relationship).

Radioimmunoassay (RIA)

4.1 What is RIA?

Radioimmunoassay (RIA) is an in vitro technique developed by Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson (which won the 1977 Nobel Prize) that uses antigen-antibody specificity together with a radioactive label (typically Iodine-125) to detect or quantify trace amounts of target molecules.

4.2 The Classic Competitive RIA Principle

Classic RIA is fundamentally a competitive binding assay. A fixed, limited quantity of antibody is mixed with a fixed amount of known radiolabelled antigen (hot antigen) and the patient's unlabelled sample antigen (cold antigen):

Known Radiolabelled Antigen (Ag*) + Unlabelled Sample Antigen (Ag) → Compete for Limited Antibody Sites → Separate Bound from Free → Measure Radioactivity
The Core Mathematical Rule of RIA:
More sample antigen → less radiolabelled antigen can bind → LOWER measured radioactivity in the bound fraction.

4.3 RIA Conceptual Workflow

Conceptually, RIA proceeds in distinct stages:

  1. Competitive Incubation: Sample antigen and known radiolabelled tracer antigen are added together to tubes containing a fixed, limited amount of specific antibody.
  2. Equilibrium Binding: Both labelled and unlabelled antigens compete to bind the limited antibody sites until chemical equilibrium is reached.
  3. Physical Separation: The antibody-antigen complexes (bound fraction) are physically separated from the remaining unattached molecules (free fraction) using chemical precipitation or solid-phase beads.
  4. Radioactivity Counting: Radioactivity of the bound fraction (or free fraction) is counted using a gamma counter or scintillation detector.
  5. Concentration Determination: The counts per minute (CPM) are compared against a standard calibration curve of known reference standards to interpolate sample concentration.

4.4 Why Was RIA So Important?

Prior to RIA, measuring minute physiological concentrations of hormones (like insulin, thyroxine, or growth hormone) in blood was virtually impossible. RIA revolutionized endocrinology and clinical biochemistry because it achieved phenomenal picogram-to-femtogram sensitivity, allowing scientists to detect ultra-trace biomolecules for the first time.

4.5 Practical Limitations

Despite its extraordinary historical sensitivity, RIA has largely been replaced by ELISA in routine clinical settings due to specific operational drawbacks:

  • Radioactive Hazard: Requires personnel exposure monitoring and radiation safety protocols.
  • Short Reagent Shelf-Life: Radioisotopes decay spontaneously (e.g., Iodine-125 has a half-life of only ~60 days), meaning reagents degrade rapidly.
  • Costly Waste Disposal: Strict regulatory laws govern the handling, containment, and disposal of radioactive waste.
  • Specialized Equipment: Requires expensive lead-shielded gamma counters or liquid scintillation counters rather than standard microplate readers.
One-Sentence Takeaway: RIA uses competitive binding between radioactive tracer and sample antigen to achieve ultra-high sensitivity, but radiation hazards and isotope decay make ELISA far more practical for everyday laboratory use.

ELISA vs Radioimmunoassay (RIA)

Neither ELISA nor RIA is universally "better" in every context. Rather, scientists choose the appropriate immunoassay based on required sensitivity, laboratory safety infrastructure, reagent stability, and regulatory convenience.

Feature ELISA (Enzyme-Linked) RIA (Radioimmunoassay)
Detection Label Enzyme (e.g., HRP, Alkaline Phosphatase) Radioactive isotope (e.g., Iodine-125, Tritium)
Signal Output Color change / Optical absorbance (OD) Ionizing radiation emissions (counts per minute, CPM)
Safety Considerations Safe; standard bio-safety level 1 or 2 rules Radiation hazard; requires shielding, dosimeters, licensing
Sensitivity High (picogram to nanogram range) Extremely high (picogram to femtogram range)
Primary Equipment Microplate spectrophotometer / reader Gamma counter or liquid scintillation counter
Waste Management Standard biochemical / laboratory waste Regulated, expensive radioactive waste disposal
Reagent Stability High shelf-life (stable enzyme conjugates for months/years) Short shelf-life (limited by isotope radioactive half-life)
Practical Convenience Very high; automated, 96-well parallel throughput Lower; requires dedicated radiation facility and precautions
Typical Conceptual Use Routine clinical diagnostics, viral antibodies, food toxins Ultra-trace peptide hormones, reference standard validation
How Method Choice is Made:
In modern hospital and academic laboratories, ELISA and its luminescent variants are chosen for >95% of routine testing due to safety, automated multi-well plate handling, and stable shelf-life. RIA remains respected as a gold-standard reference method when ultra-sensitive trace hormone quantification is essential and radiation infrastructure exists.

The Big Picture

Every immunoassay in biochemistry and microbiology boils down to one unifying roadmap:

ANTIGEN + ANTIBODY SPECIFICITY → IMMUNOASSAY → MEASURABLE SIGNAL
ELISA → ENZYME LABEL → COLOR / ABSORBANCE (Non-radioactive, enzyme-based detection)
RIA → RADIOACTIVE LABEL → RADIOACTIVITY (CPM) (Radioactive-label-based detection)

If You Remember Only Five Things

  • Antibodies recognize specific targets: High-affinity, lock-and-key recognition between paratopes and epitopes allows selective detection of target molecules within complex biological samples.
  • ELISA converts recognition into an enzyme-generated measurable signal: An immobilized antibody or antigen captures the target, and an attached enzyme converts substrate into a quantifiable color change (absorbance).
  • Direct, indirect, sandwich, and competitive ELISA differ mainly in arrangement: The four major formats vary by how antigens, primary antibodies, secondary antibodies, and enzyme labels are assembled on the well surface.
  • RIA uses a radioactive label, commonly through competitive binding: Unlabelled patient antigen competes with known radiolabelled tracer antigen for limited antibody binding sites.
  • Measured signal interpretation depends on assay design: More signal does NOT universally mean more target. Direct/sandwich ELISAs show a direct relationship, whereas competitive ELISA and competitive RIA show an inverse relationship.

SECTION 01 • SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATIONS

What is Scanning Electron Microscopy?

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is a premier analytical technique engineered to acquire high-resolution imagery and detailed topography, morphology, and elemental composition of specimens. Rather than utilizing visible photons, an SEM projects and rasters an energetic, focused beam of electrons across a specimen’s surface inside a controlled vacuum chamber.

“The electrons in the beam interact with the atoms within the sample, thereby producing various signals that can be used to obtain information about the surface’s topography and composition. Images are viewed in real-time on an external monitor using software that correlates the beam’s position with the intensity of electrons acquired by the detector(s).”

— Nanoscience Instruments, Scanning Electron Microscopy
🎯

Sub-Nanometer Resolution

Standard optical light microscopy is bounded by optical diffraction to approximately 200–250 nm. In contrast, modern SEM instruments achieve spatial resolution ranging from < 1 nanometer up to several nanometers, unlocking true nanoscale characterization.

⚡

Multi-Modal Signal Detection

The electron-specimen interaction produces a rich cascade of complementary signals simultaneously: Secondary Electrons (SE) for surface topography, Backscattered Electrons (BSE) for compositional atomic number contrast, and Characteristic X-rays (EDS) for quantitative elemental fingerprinting.

SECTION 02 • THE WAVE NATURE OF LIGHT & ELECTRONS

Why Use Electrons Instead of Light in a Microscope?

Every optical instrument is fundamentally constrained by the wave nature of light. When light waves pass through the circular aperture of an objective lens, diffraction causes the rays to spread and interfere, projecting a point source not as an infinitesimal point, but as an Airy diffraction pattern consisting of a central bright disk surrounded by concentric dark and bright rings.

Rayleigh Diffraction-Limited Spatial Resolution Criterion:
$$\delta = \frac{0.61 \cdot \lambda}{\mu \sin\beta} = \frac{0.61 \cdot \lambda}{\text{NA}}$$

$\delta$ = Smallest resolvable distance (spatial resolution limit)

$\lambda$ = Incident radiation wavelength

$\mu$ = Refractive index of viewing medium (air = 1.0, oil = 1.515)

$\beta$ = Semi-angle of the objective lens aperture collection cone

$\text{NA} = \mu \sin\beta$ = Numerical Aperture of the objective

As established by Ernst Abbe and Lord Rayleigh, simplifying the numerical aperture ($\text{NA}$) to approximately 1.0 yields the universal empirical rule of light microscopy: the theoretical resolution limit is roughly half of the incident illumination wavelength ($\delta \approx \lambda / 2$). For visible green light ($\lambda = 550\text{ nm}$), two specimen features closer than $\sim 275\text{ nm}$ blur together irrevocably into a single diffraction blob.

SECTION 03 • QUANTUM PHYSICS OF ELECTRON BEAMS

Wavelength of a Fast Electron & The de Broglie Principle

In 1924, French physicist Louis de Broglie postulated wave-particle duality: all moving matter possesses an associated wave-like nature. When an electron with rest mass $m$ and elementary charge $e$ is accelerated through an electrostatic potential difference $V$ (accelerating voltage), it acquires kinetic energy $E_k = e V$. Equating kinetic energy to momentum ($p = \sqrt{2 m e V}$) yields the de Broglie wavelength:

de Broglie Electron Wavelength Equation:
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m e V}}$$

$h$ = Planck’s constant ($6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{m}^2\cdot\text{kg/s}$)

$m$ = Electron rest mass ($9.109 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$)

$e$ = Elementary electron charge ($1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$)

$V$ = Accelerating voltage applied to the electron gun (Volts, V)

Because Planck's constant is tiny ($10^{-34}$), accelerating electrons across kilovolts compresses their wavelength down to fractions of an Ångström—over 100,000 times shorter than visible light!

Crucial Scientific Insight: Why Doesn’t an SEM Resolve at 0.01 nm?

Students frequently ask: “If the electron wavelength at 10 kV is 0.0122 nm, why is SEM resolution typically 1 to 4 nm, and not 0.01 nm?”

As highlighted by Nanoscience Instruments: In practice, diffraction-limited resolution is never achieved in electron optics because of electromagnetic lens imperfections (aberrations) and the finite beam spot size.

  • Spherical Aberration ($C_s$): Electrons passing through the outer periphery of a magnetic lens are bent more strongly than axial electrons, blurring a mathematical point into a disc of confusion.
  • Chromatic Aberration ($C_c$): Thermal energy spread from the emitter ($\Delta E$) causes electrons of slightly different energies to focus at different focal planes.
  • Interaction Volume: The primary beam scatters laterally beneath the surface over tens of nanometers to micrometers, broadening the emission volume of exit signals.
SECTION 04 • DIRECT COMPARATIVE MICROSCOPY

Optical Microscope vs. Scanning Electron Microscope

Beyond superior spatial resolution, the scanning electron microscope possesses another transformative advantage over optical microscopy: an immense depth of field.

Depth of field (DOF) defines the axial range along the optical axis over which specimen structures appear reasonably in focus simultaneously without perceptible blurring. In light microscopy, the objective lens must possess a wide aperture collection angle ($\beta$) to collect enough light and maximize NA. However, a wide convergence cone forces a very steep focal wedge: move even 0.5 µm up or down, and the image becomes severely blurred.

In an SEM, the electron beam is focused into an almost parallel pencil beam with an extremely tiny aperture semi-angle ($\alpha \approx 2\text{ to }10\text{ milliradians} \approx 0.1^\circ\text{ to }0.6^\circ$). Because the beam is nearly parallel, it remains narrow over several micrometers or millimeters of vertical depth:

Depth of Field Geometric Approximation:
$$D_{\text{field}} \approx \frac{\delta_{\text{pixel}}}{\tan\alpha} \approx \frac{d_{\text{probe}}}{\alpha}$$

$D_{\text{field}}$ = Depth of field (vertical distance in focus)

$d_{\text{probe}}$ = Electron probe spot diameter on the specimen

$\alpha$ = Beam convergence semi-angle formed by the final objective aperture

Case Study: Electrospun Filtration Nanofibers

Electrospun polymer nanofibers used in air and water filtration form an intricate three-dimensional fibrous mesh. The figures below compare the identical nanofiber layer imaged under an optical microscope versus an SEM at 4,000× magnification:

Optical Analysis: Only fibers directly intersecting the focal plane are sharp. Foreground and background fibers dissolve into cloudy white halos. Accurate measurements of fiber diameters, pore sizes, and branching junctions are impossible without taking dozens of optical z-slices.

SEM Analysis: Crisp, needle-sharp focus is maintained through the entire depth of the 3D matrix. Fine individual fibers, web interconnects, and nanopores are distinct with rich topographic contrast, enabling automated dimensional analysis.

SECTION 06 • ELECTRON EMISSION PHYSICS

Electron Sources: Tungsten, Hexaboride Crystals & Field Emission Guns

The electron source is the heartbeat of the scanning electron microscope. As emphasized by Nanoscience Instruments, the emitter governs analytical brightness, beam coherence, probe diameter, and ultimate spatial resolution. SEMs utilize three prevalent emitter technologies:

1. Tungsten (W) Filament

An inverted V-shaped wire (~100 µm diameter) heated resistively to ~2800 K. Low cost and forgiving vacuum requirements ($10^{-1}$ to $10^{-5}$ mbar). However, rapid tungsten evaporation causes column contamination and sudden burnout after ~100 hours of operation. Sensitive to thermal fluctuations, requiring frequent stigmation adjustment.

2. Cerium / Lanthanum Hexaboride

Single-crystal solid-state tip ($\text{CeB}_6$ or $\text{LaB}_6$) with a flat or truncated apex (~25 µm). Because hexaboride crystals have a low work function (~2.6 eV vs 4.5 eV for W), they operate at ~1800 K with ~10× higher brightness, 1,500 hour operational lifetime, and cleaner column stability.

3. Schottky Field Emission Gun (FEG)

Monocrystalline tungsten wire etched into an atomic tip (<100 nm radius) coated with Zirconium oxide ($\text{ZrO}_2$). Employs field-assisted electron tunneling: an extraction anode pulls electrons via quantum tunneling while an electrostatic lens collimates them. Unmatched brightness, minimum chromatic spread, and >10,000 hour lifespan.

SECTION 07 • ELECTRON OPTICAL DEMAGNIFICATION

Beam Diameter, Demagnification & Spot Size Engineering

The electron source emits an electron cloud spanning tens of micrometers. If projected directly onto the specimen, this broad spot would yield terrible spatial resolution. To produce a fine analytical probe ($< 1\text{ nm}$ to $20\text{ nm}$), the SEM column acts as a demagnifying electron-optical telescope.

Electromagnetic condenser lenses use copper solenoids to generate toroidal magnetic fields. By increasing the electrical current passing through the condenser solenoid, the focal length shortens and the beam crossover moves closer to the lens. High-angle peripheral electrons strike the physical metallic condenser aperture and are stripped away:

Total Electron Probe Spot Size ($d_p$):
$$d_p = \sqrt{d_g^2 \cdot M_{\text{tot}}^2 + d_{\text{diff}}^2 + d_{\text{sph}}^2 + d_{\text{chr}}^2}$$

$d_g \cdot M_{\text{tot}}$ = Demagnified geometric image of the electron gun source

$d_{\text{diff}} = 0.61 \lambda / \alpha$ = Wave diffraction disc diameter

$d_{\text{sph}} = \frac{1}{2} C_s \alpha^3$ = Spherical aberration disc of confusion

$d_{\text{chr}} = C_c \alpha (\Delta E / E_0)$ = Chromatic aberration disc

SECTION 08 • VACUUM ENGINEERING

Vacuum System Architecture: Mean Free Path & Differentially Pumped Chambers

A high vacuum is not merely an operational convenience in an SEM—it is an absolute physical necessity. Operating an electron microscope at atmospheric pressure is impossible for two primary reasons:

💨

1. Electron Scattering & Mean Free Path

At atmospheric pressure, an electron cannot travel more than a fraction of a micrometer before violently colliding with nitrogen or oxygen gas molecules. In a vacuum of $10^{-5}\text{ mbar}$, the electron mean free path ($\lambda_{\text{mfp}}$) exceeds 100 meters—vastly longer than the 0.5 to 1.0-meter column length—guaranteeing that electrons travel unimpeded from gun to specimen.

🔥

2. Source Lifetime & High-Voltage Arcing

Heated tungsten or hexaboride filaments would burn out and oxidize within milliseconds if exposed to trace atmospheric oxygen ($O_2$). Furthermore, applying 30 kV across an anode gap in air would cause catastrophic dielectric electrical breakdown and violent lightning arcs.

SECTION 09 • IMAGE FORMATION & DEFLECTION

Scanning Coils & The Raster Scan Process

Unlike an optical microscope or transmission electron microscope (TEM) where an entire field of view is projected simultaneously onto a detector array, the SEM forms an image sequentially, point-by-point, pixel-by-pixel in time.

Two orthogonal pairs of electromagnetic coils—the X and Y scanning coils located within or directly above the objective lens—deflect the electron beam in a precise rectilinear raster pattern across the specimen surface. As the beam visits each coordinate $(X, Y)$, emitted electrons are gathered by a detector. SECTION 10 • DIGITAL SCANNING GEOMETRY

Magnification: Pure Geometry, Not Optical Lenses

In an optical microscope, changing magnification requires physically rotating an objective lens turret to swap glass optics of different focal lengths. In an SEM, magnification is purely geometric: no physical lenses move.

The magnification of an SEM is defined simply as the linear ratio between the physical width of the display monitor and the physical width of the area scanned on the specimen by the electron beam:

Fundamental SEM Magnification Equation:
$$M = \frac{L_{\text{display}}}{L_{\text{scan}}}$$

$M$ = Magnification factor (e.g. 5,000×)

$L_{\text{display}}$ = Physical width of the viewing screen / image raster (fixed, typically 100 mm / 10 cm)

$L_{\text{scan}}$ = Physical horizontal width of the area scanned on the specimen

To achieve higher magnification, the scan generator simply reduces the deflection currents driving the scan coils. The electron beam sweeps over a progressively smaller patch of the specimen surface, while the display monitor continues mapping those coordinates to the same fixed monitor width!

SECTION 11 • SCATTERING PHYSICS

Sample-Electron Interactions & The Interaction Volume

When the primary electron beam impinges upon a solid target, the electrons do not halt at the immediate surface. Instead, they penetrate into the material, undergoing thousands of successive elastic scattering collisions (deflection by atomic nuclei) and inelastic scattering events (energy transfer to atomic electrons).

This scattering cascade spreads the incident beam laterally and axially, generating a distinct pear-shaped or teardrop-shaped interaction volume beneath the impact point:

The maximum depth of electron penetration into the specimen is modeled by the Kanaya-Okayama range equation ($R_{\text{KO}}$):

Kanaya-Okayama Penetration Range Equation:
$$R_{\text{KO}} = \frac{0.0276 \cdot A}{\rho \cdot Z^{0.89}} \cdot E_0^{1.67}\quad [\mu\text{m}]$$

$E_0$ = Primary beam accelerating energy (in keV)

$A$ = Atomic weight of specimen material (g/mol)

$\rho$ = Specimen physical mass density ($\text{g/cm}^3$)

$Z$ = Average atomic number of specimen atoms

SECTION 12 • TOPOGRAPHICAL CONTRAST

Secondary Electrons (SE): High-Resolution Surface Morphology

Secondary electrons (SE) are the most widely utilized signal in scanning electron microscopy for capturing high-resolution three-dimensional images of specimen surfaces.

Physics of Secondary Electron Generation

A secondary electron is emitted as a direct consequence of inelastic scattering: a primary beam electron collides with a weakly bound valence or conduction electron of a specimen atom, transferring a fraction of its kinetic energy. By formal physical definition, secondary electrons possess kinetic energies of less than 50 eV (typically 2 to 5 eV).

Why SE Provides the Best Spatial Resolution

Because their energy is so low (<50 eV), secondary electrons generated deeper than 5 to 10 nm undergo rapid inelastic reabsorption and cannot escape the sample. Only secondary electrons generated in the topmost escape layer (< 10 nm) escape into the vacuum. Consequently, the SE emission zone matches the tight diameter of the primary beam spot!

The Edge Effect & Topographical Shading

Secondary electron yield is highly sensitive to the local angle of incidence ($\theta$) of the specimen surface relative to the beam ($I_{\text{SE}} \propto \sec\theta$). When the beam hits a flat, perpendicular surface, electrons generated at depth must travel straight up to escape. However, when the beam strikes a sharp edge, corner, or steep incline, the escape distance to the nearby flank is drastically reduced, allowing far more secondary electrons to emerge into the vacuum.

This edge effect causes edges, tips, and ridges to glow brightly, endowing SEM images with their unmistakable, intuitively interpretable three-dimensional depth.

SECTION 13 • COMPOSITIONAL PHASE CONTRAST

Backscattered Electrons (BSE): Atomic Number (Z) Contrast & Multi-Quadrant Detectors

While secondary electrons reveal surface shape, backscattered electrons (BSE) reveal what the specimen is physically made of.

Backscattered electrons are primary beam electrons that undergo elastic scattering at high angles (> 90°) caused by the strong positive Coulomb attraction of specimen atomic nuclei. Unlike secondary electrons, backscattered electrons retain the vast majority of their original beam energy (up to tens of keV) and emerge from deeper sub-surface depths.

Backscatter Electron Coefficient ($\eta$) vs Atomic Number ($Z$):
$$\eta \approx \frac{\ln Z}{6} - \frac{1}{4}\quad (\text{for }Z > 10)$$

$\eta$ = Backscatter yield ratio ($I_{\text{BSE}} / I_{\text{beam}}$)

$Z$ = Atomic number of specimen atoms

Physical rule: Heavier elements with larger nuclear charge $Z$ deflect dramatically more electrons back into the detector, appearing distinctly brighter than lighter elements.

SECTION 14 • ELEMENTAL MICROANALYSIS

Characteristic X-Rays & Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)

When primary electrons collide with specimen atoms, they occasionally knock out an electron from an tightly bound inner shell (such as the K-shell or L-shell), leaving the atom in an unstable, ionized state.

Within femtoseconds, an electron from a higher-energy outer shell drops down to fill the vacancy. The discrete difference in binding energy between the two quantum shells is conserved and emitted as an electromagnetic photon: a Characteristic X-ray:

Characteristic X-Ray Quantum Emission:
$$\Delta E = E_{\text{outer}} - E_{\text{inner}} = h \cdot \nu = \frac{h \cdot c}{\lambda_X}$$

$\Delta E$ = Characteristic energy unique to the atomic number of each specific element

$h$ = Planck’s constant

$\nu$ = X-ray photon frequency

Silicon Drift Detectors (SDD) & The Beryllium Window

Modern SEMs employ Silicon Drift Detectors (SDD). When an X-ray enters the reverse-biased high-purity silicon crystal, it generates a cloud of electron-hole pairs (one pair per 3.8 eV of X-ray energy). An annular drift ring electric field sweeps the electrons to a microscopic central anode, minimizing detector capacitance and enabling counting rates exceeding 1,000,000 counts per second.

As noted by Nanoscience Instruments: “Most SDDs contain a thin window... Beryllium windows are robust but do absorb X-rays from elements up to Be, meaning that elements from Boron ($Z \ge 5$) and of higher atomic numbers can still be detected.”

SECTION 15 • BACKGROUND RADIATION

Continuum X-Rays: Bremsstrahlung Background & The Duane-Hunt Limit

In addition to discrete characteristic elemental peaks, every EDS spectrum features a continuous broad background known as Bremsstrahlung (German for braking radiation).

When an incident electron passes near the strong electric field of a specimen atomic nucleus without ejecting an orbital electron, it undergoes rapid Coulomb deceleration. By Maxwell's classical electrodynamics, an accelerating or decelerating electric charge must radiate electromagnetic energy. Because this braking occurs at arbitrary impact distances, photons of all energies are emitted continuously:

The Duane-Hunt High-Energy Cutoff Limit:
$$E_{\text{max}} = e \cdot V_{\text{acc}} = \frac{h \cdot c}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}$$

$E_{\text{max}}$ = Highest possible X-ray energy in the spectrum, exactly equal to the incident primary electron energy ($E_0$)

Practical Application: The Duane-Hunt limit provides a foolproof internal calibration check in EDS: the spectrum background drops precisely to zero at exactly the column accelerating voltage (e.g. 15.0 keV).

Analytical Impact: Peak-to-Background Ratio ($P/B$)

Bremsstrahlung continuum forms the baseline "noise" beneath characteristic elemental peaks. To detect trace elements (< 0.1 wt%), the characteristic peak must rise statistically above the local Bremsstrahlung continuum. Bremsstrahlung intensity is proportional to specimen atomic number ($Z$) and beam current ($I_{\text{beam}}$).

SECTION 16 • INSTRUMENT HARDWARE

Detector Architectures: Everhart-Thornley, Solid-State BSD & SDD

Because the electron-specimen interaction produces multiple distinct emission signals with vastly different energies, an SEM employs specialized detector architectures strategically positioned around the specimen:

1. Everhart-Thornley (E-T) SED

The classic secondary electron detector. Positioned to one side of the specimen. Encased in a wire-mesh Faraday cage biased to +200 to +300 V to draw in slow (<50 eV) secondary electrons. Once inside, electrons are accelerated toward a plastic scintillator disk biased to +10 kV. Photons emitted by the scintillator travel down a light guide into a photomultiplier tube (PMT), producing an amplified electrical signal.

2. Solid-State Annular BSD

Mounted directly beneath the pole piece of the objective lens, centered symmetrically above the specimen. Contains silicon p-n semiconductor junction diodes. Energetic backscattered electrons (>5 keV) plunge into the silicon, generating electron-hole pairs. Operates effectively even at low vacuum levels (0.1–1 mbar) where high-voltage scintillators would ignite electrical discharges.

3. Silicon Drift Detector (SDD)

Positioned at a precise take-off angle (~35°). Contains an active silicon crystal protected by an ultra-thin Beryllium or polymer window. When characteristic X-ray photons enter, they ionize electron-hole pairs proportional to photon energy. Concentric ring electrodes guide electrons to a sub-picofarad central anode, enabling ultra-fast, high-count-rate elemental mapping.

SECTION 19 • SAMPLE PREPARATION & ARTIFACT MITIGATION

Biological Materials & Insulating Samples: Charging, Beam Damage & Sputter Coating

Unlike metals and semiconductors, biological specimens (cells, bacteria, plant tissues) and electrical insulators (ceramics, polymers, glasses) do not possess a continuous pathway to ground electrical charges.

⚡

The Electrical Charging Phenomenon

When an insulating sample is bombarded by high-energy electrons in a high vacuum, absorbed electrons accumulate in the dielectric material. As negative charge builds up, a severe negative electrostatic potential develops on the surface. This creates:

  • Blinding White Flares: Violent deflection and acceleration of secondary electrons into the detector, saturating pixels to pure white.
  • Severe Image Distortion & Drift: The electrostatic field bends the incoming primary beam mid-air, causing lines to shear and warp.
  • Dielectric Arcing: Sudden catastrophic electrical discharge sparks across the sample surface.
🛡️

Three Proven Engineering Solutions

  1. Sputter Coating: Depositing an ultra-thin (2 to 10 nm) layer of conducting metal (Gold, Gold/Palladium, or Platinum) or evaporated Carbon in a low-pressure Argon plasma. Forms a grounded conductive skin that drains accumulated charge.
  2. Low-Vacuum / Variable Pressure (VP-SEM): Operating at 0.1 to 1.0 mbar water vapor or nitrogen. Primary and secondary electrons collide with gas molecules, producing positive gas ions that drift to the sample and neutralize negative surface charge.
  3. Low-kV Imaging ($E_2$ Crossover): Tuning beam energy to 1.0–1.5 keV where the total electron emission yield ($\sigma + \eta = 1.0$). Incoming charge exactly equals outgoing charge—zero net charging!

FAULT CODE: OPT-01

Astigmatism (Objective Lens Asymmetry)

Observed Symptom: Image stretches horizontally when focusing through one side of focus, and stretches vertically when focusing through the other side. Circular nanoparticles appear oval.

Physical Root Cause: Non-uniform magnetic field in the objective lens pole piece caused by aperture contamination or machining tolerances. The electron probe is elliptical rather than circular.

Prescribed Operator Fix: Adjust the X and Y Objective Stigmators (octupole electromagnetic coils) at high magnification while rocking focus until features stretch symmetrically in all directions.
SECTION 21 • SCIENTIFIC METROLOGY

Resolution vs. Magnification: The "Empty Magnification" Trap

One of the most dangerous misconceptions in microscopy is equating magnification with resolution.

True Spatial Resolution ($\delta$)

Resolution is an immutable physical property of the electron probe diameter, accelerating voltage, lens aberrations, and specimen interaction volume. It defines the minimum physical distance separating two distinct specimen features such that they can be distinguished as separate entities.

Magnification ($M$)

Magnification is purely an enlargement factor ($L_{\text{display}} / L_{\text{scan}}$). Any digital computer can increase magnification to 1,000,000× simply by displaying a tiny scanned region across a larger screen.

The Concept of "Empty Magnification"

When an operator increases magnification beyond the physical resolving limit of the instrument, no additional specimen information is revealed. Instead, the operator merely magnifies blurred probe discs or displays chunky digital pixels.

Maximum Useful Magnification ($M_{\text{useful}}$):
$$M_{\text{useful}} \approx \frac{\delta_{\text{eye}}}{\delta_{\text{SEM}}} \approx \frac{0.2\text{ mm}}{\delta_{\text{SEM}}}$$

For an SEM with $2.0\text{ nm}$ practical resolution, $M_{\text{useful}} \approx \frac{0.2\times 10^{-3}\text{ m}}{2\times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} \approx 100,000\times$. Zooming to 500,000× without improving probe diameter is empty magnification.

Pedagogical Guide: How to Read an SEM Micrograph Data Bar

Every scientific SEM micrograph features an calibrated informational data bar along the bottom containing critical imaging parameters:

  • Scale Bar (e.g. 10 µm): The physical reference standard. Never report magnification without a scale bar, because image scaling alters apparent magnification!
  • $V_{\text{acc}}$ / HV (e.g. 15.0 kV): Accelerating voltage. Lower values (1–5 kV) accentuate fine surface topography; higher values (15–20 kV) are required for EDS excitation.
  • Detector (e.g. SED / BSD): Indicates whether contrast represents surface height (SE) or atomic number composition (BSE).
  • WD (Working Distance, e.g. 7.5 mm): Distance from pole piece to specimen. Shorter WD yields higher resolution; longer WD affords greater depth of field.

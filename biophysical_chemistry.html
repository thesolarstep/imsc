Principles of Biophysical Chemistry

The physics and chemistry that quietly runs every microbe: how acidic its cytoplasm is, how it buffers shock, how fast its reactions run, why some processes happen on their own, and how it fights to keep water where it needs it.

01

Why microbiologists need biophysical chemistry

Every organism you study under the microscope is, underneath, a bag of chemistry obeying physical rules. This session gives you the four rules that matter most in the lab and the clinic.

Biophysical chemistryThe branch of chemistry that uses the tools of physics — energy, rates, equilibrium, concentration — to explain how biological molecules and cells behave. is not a separate subject from microbiology; it is the reason microbiology works the way it does.

Where you already use it without naming it

Every time you check a culture medium's pH before autoclaving, dilute an antibiotic stock, watch a growth curve, or explain why a cell shrivels in strong saline — you are doing biophysical chemistry.

What this class covers

Four pillars: pH & buffers (keeping the internal environment stable), kinetics (how fast reactions and drug action occur), thermodynamics (why reactions happen at all), and colligative properties (how cells manage water and solutes).

🎯 Exam Tip
Examiners love asking "explain in one line why this matters to a microbiologist." Each section below ends with exactly that kind of one-liner — memorise those first, formulas second.

Mathematics Refresher (read this before the formulas)

Nothing below needs anything beyond what you learned before Class 10. We are simply going to use it in a chemistry sentence instead of a maths sentence.

Scientific notation — writing very small / very large numbers

A bacterial cell's hydrogen ion concentration is often written as 0.0000001 mol/L. That's clumsy. Scientific notation just counts how many places the decimal moved: 1 × 10⁻⁷. The small raised number (exponent) after the 10 tells you the direction and count of the shift. Negative exponent = a small number (decimal moved right); positive exponent = a large number (decimal moved left).

Powers — what the small raised number means

10² means "10 multiplied by itself twice" = 100. 10⁻² means "1 divided by 10 twice" = 0.01. That's all a power is: repeated multiplication (positive exponent) or repeated division (negative exponent).

Logarithms from zero — the only thing you need for pH

A logarithm answers one question: "10 raised to what power gives me this number?" If a number is 10⁻⁷, its log is simply −7 (the exponent, unchanged). If a number is 0.001 (=10⁻³), its log is −3. That is the entire operation used to calculate pH — you are not "doing calculus," you are just reading off the exponent.

Why chemists bother with logs at all: hydrogen ion concentration in living systems ranges from 1 to 0.0000000000001 mol/L — fourteen decimal places. No one can compare numbers like that at a glance. Taking the log compresses that huge range into a simple, friendly scale of 0 to 14.

Negative signs — why pH has a minus in front

Because H⁺ concentrations are always fractions less than 1, their logarithm is always negative (e.g. log of 10⁻⁷ = −7). Chemists find negative numbers annoying to talk about ("the pH is minus seven"?), so they simply flip the sign in the definition: pH = −log[H⁺]. The minus sign in the formula exists purely to cancel the minus sign that the log naturally produces, giving you a clean positive number.

Fractions and ratios — what "[base]/[acid]" means

A fraction such as [A⁻]/[HA] is just "how much of the base form exists for every unit of the acid form." If it equals 1, there's exactly equal amounts of both. If it's greater than 1, base dominates; less than 1, acid dominates. That single ratio is the heart of the buffer equation you'll meet shortly.

Rearranging an equation — moving terms across the "=" sign

Whatever you do to one side of "=", you must do to the other, to keep both sides equal. If C = A × B, and you want B alone, divide both sides by A: B = C/A. Every "rearranged" formula in this class is produced by this one rule, applied patiently, one step at a time.

Unit conversions — keeping your numbers honest

1 mol/L = 1000 mmol/L. 1 L = 1000 mL. Before plugging a number into any formula, check that every quantity is in the unit the formula expects — this single habit prevents most numerical mistakes in exams.

✅ Remember This
"If concentration doubles…", "if pH becomes smaller…", "if ΔG is negative…" — every formula in this class will be explained in this plain, directional language before you ever see the symbols.

pH

The single number that tells you how acidic or alkaline a solution is — and one of the tightest-controlled variables inside any living cell.

Definition

pH"Power of Hydrogen" — a scale that expresses the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution. measures the concentration of free hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution. More free H⁺ = more acidic = lower pH. Fewer free H⁺ = more alkaline (basic) = higher pH.

The pH scale

pH = −log₁₀ [H⁺]
SymbolMeaningUnit
pHAcidity/alkalinity value read on the 0–14 scalenone (it's a log ratio)
[H⁺]Molar concentration of hydrogen ions ("square brackets" always mean "concentration of")mol/L
log₁₀"What power of 10 gives this number" — see Math Refresher—

Why logarithms, specifically, are used here

[H⁺] in biological fluids spans from about 1 mol/L (strong stomach acid) down to 0.0000000000001 mol/L (strong alkali) — a range of 14 orders of magnitude. A log scale converts that unmanageable spread into simple whole numbers, and it also matches how our instruments (and our biology) actually respond: each one-unit change in pH is a tenfold change in acidity, not a small step.

⚠️ Common Mistake
Students think pH 4 is "twice as acidic" as pH 8. It is not — it is 10,000 times more acidic (four steps of ×10 each), because the scale is logarithmic, not linear.

Worked example

A fermenting Lactobacillus culture has [H⁺] = 0.0001 mol/L. Find its pH.

  1. Write in scientific notation: 0.0001 = 1 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L
  2. Take log₁₀ of this number — by the rule in the Math Refresher, the log of 10⁻⁴ is simply its exponent: log₁₀(10⁻⁴) = −4
  3. Apply the formula: pH = −(−4) = 4

Answer: pH 4 — clearly acidic, consistent with lactic acid fermentation.

🧫 Microbiology Connection
Lactobacillus fermented foods (yogurt, sauerkorn) drop to pH ~4, which is exactly what suppresses spoilage organisms.
🩺 Clinical Connection
Human blood is tightly held at pH 7.35–7.45; a shift of even 0.3 units (acidosis/alkalosis) can be life-threatening.
🎯 Exam Tip
If asked "why is pH logarithmic," always mention both reasons: the huge concentration range, AND the tenfold-per-unit relationship.

Buffer Systems

How a cell keeps its internal pH nearly constant even when acid or base is added or produced.

The building blocks

Weak acidAn acid that only partially releases its H⁺ into solution, leaving an equilibrium between the intact acid and its ions. — does not fully release its H⁺; some stays bound, some is free. This partial, reversible release is exactly what makes it useful as a buffer component.

Conjugate baseWhat remains of a weak acid after it has donated its H⁺. — the leftover part of the weak acid after it has given up H⁺. Together, the weak acid and its conjugate base form a "buffer pair."

Weak baseA base that only partially accepts H⁺ from solution. — only partially grabs H⁺ from solution, leaving room for it to grab more if acid is added.

Conjugate acidWhat a weak base becomes after accepting an H⁺. — what the weak base becomes once it has accepted H⁺.

How a buffer actually works (the mechanism, in words)

A buffer solution contains both members of a weak-acid/conjugate-base pair at the same time. If acid (extra H⁺) is added, the conjugate base soaks it up, converting into the weak acid form. If base (or something that removes H⁺) is added, the weak acid donates H⁺ to replace what was lost. Either way, the pool of free H⁺ barely changes — the pH holds steady.

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

pH = pKa + log₁₀ ( [A⁻] / [HA] )
SymbolMeaningUnit
pHResulting acidity of the buffer mixturenone
pKaThe pH at which the acid is exactly half-dissociated — a fixed property of each weak acid (like a fingerprint)none
[A⁻]Concentration of the conjugate base formmol/L
[HA]Concentration of the undissociated weak acid formmol/L

What changes if [A⁻] increases (more base form): the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] rises, its log becomes more positive, so pH rises (more alkaline). What changes if [HA] increases (more acid form): the ratio falls, its log becomes more negative, so pH falls (more acidic). When [A⁻] = [HA], the ratio is 1, log(1) = 0, so pH = pKa exactly — this is the buffer's most effective point.

Worked example

A phosphate buffer has pKa = 6.8, with [A⁻] = 0.02 M and [HA] = 0.01 M. Find the pH.

  1. Form the ratio: [A⁻]/[HA] = 0.02/0.01 = 2
  2. Take log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.30
  3. Add to pKa: pH = 6.8 + 0.30 = 7.1

Answer: pH ≈ 7.1 — right in the physiological range, which is exactly why phosphate is a key intracellular buffer.

Three biological buffers you must know

Bicarbonate

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Dominant buffer of blood plasma; the lungs adjust CO₂ to fine-tune it breath by breath.

Phosphate

H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻. pKa ~6.8, close to intracellular pH — the main buffer inside cells and in urine.

Proteins

Side chains of amino acids (e.g. histidine) can accept or donate H⁺, giving proteins their own built-in buffering capacity, including in blood via hemoglobin.

🧫 Microbiology Connection
Culture media are pre-buffered (often with phosphate) precisely so that microbial acid waste products don't crash the pH and kill the very organisms you're trying to grow.

Reaction Kinetics

How fast a reaction happens — and why that speed matters just as much as whether it happens at all.

What "reaction rate" means

Reaction rateHow quickly the concentration of a reactant falls, or a product rises, per unit time. is simply: how much concentration changes, divided by how much time passed. A fast rate means the graph of concentration vs. time drops (or rises) steeply; a slow rate means it's nearly flat.

Zero-order

Rate = k

Rate stays constant — independent of how much substrate is left. Concentration falls in a straight line. Occurs when an enzyme is fully saturated with substrate.

First-order

Rate = k[A]

Rate is directly proportional to the amount of substrate remaining — as substrate drops, rate slows. Concentration falls in a smooth curve. Most drug elimination follows this pattern.

Second-order

Rate = k[A]²

Rate depends on the square of concentration (or the product of two reactant concentrations) — doubling concentration quadruples the rate. Falls even more steeply at first, then levels off.

The rate constant, k

k is a fixed number for a given reaction under given conditions (temperature, catalyst) that tells you how intrinsically fast that reaction is, independent of how much reactant you started with. Its unit changes with order: zero-order → mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (concentration per time); first-order → s⁻¹ (per time only); second-order → L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Half-life (t½)

Half-lifeThe time needed for a reactant's concentration to fall to half of its starting value. tells you, in one simple number, how long a process takes — useful for drug dosing intervals and for predicting how long a disinfectant stays effective. For a first-order process, t½ = 0.693/k, and — importantly — it is constant, the same no matter what the starting concentration is. For zero-order, t½ depends on the starting concentration (it's not constant).

🩺 Clinical Connection
Most drugs are eliminated from the body by first-order kinetics — this is why a drug's half-life (e.g. "8 hours") is quoted as a single fixed number regardless of dose.
🧫 Microbiology Connection
Enzyme-catalysed reactions (e.g. penicillinase breaking down penicillin) shift from first-order at low substrate to zero-order once the enzyme is saturated — this transition is the basis of Michaelis–Menten kinetics you'll meet later.

Worked example

A drug is eliminated by first-order kinetics with k = 0.0693 hr⁻¹. Find its half-life.

  1. Use the formula: t½ = 0.693 / k
  2. Substitute: t½ = 0.693 / 0.0693
  3. Divide: t½ = 10 hours

Answer: 10 hours — the drug's concentration halves every 10 hours, regardless of the starting dose.

⚠️ Common Mistake
Students assume half-life is always constant. It is only constant for first-order processes — for zero-order, a bigger starting dose takes a proportionally longer time to halve.

Thermodynamics

The rules that decide whether a reaction can happen on its own — and how much usable energy it releases or requires.

The vocabulary, one term at a time

SystemThe specific reaction or process you are studying — e.g., one enzyme reaction inside a cell. — the part of the universe you are actually studying (e.g., a single reaction inside a cell).

SurroundingsEverything outside the system that can exchange energy with it. — literally everything else, which can exchange heat or energy with the system.

Enthalpy (H)The total heat content of a system at constant pressure. — a bookkeeping measure of the heat content of a system.

Entropy (S)A measure of disorder or the number of ways energy/matter can be arranged. — a measure of disorder/randomness; nature tends to move toward higher entropy.

EquilibriumThe state where forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, so no further net change occurs. — the point where a reaction's forward and backward rates are equal, so nothing net changes anymore.

Gibbs Free Energy — the master decision-maker

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
SymbolMeaningUnit
ΔGChange in free (usable) energy — the deciding number for spontaneitykJ/mol
ΔHChange in heat content (negative = heat released)kJ/mol
TAbsolute temperatureKelvin (K)
ΔSChange in disorder (positive = more disordered products)kJ/mol·K

Line by line: ΔH tells you the heat side of the story; TΔS tells you the "disorder x temperature" side; ΔG combines both into one verdict. If ΔG is negative, the reaction releases usable energy and proceeds on its own — this is called spontaneousHappens on its own, without a continuous outside energy input — says nothing about speed.. If ΔG is positive, the reaction needs an external energy input to proceed. If ΔG is zero, the system is at equilibrium.

⚠️ Common Mistake
"Spontaneous" does NOT mean "instant" or "fast." A spontaneous reaction can still be extremely slow (that's kinetics, a separate question) — ΔG only tells you it *can* happen without outside energy, not *how quickly*.

Reaction energy landscape

The hump is the activation energyThe energy barrier that must be crossed before a reaction can proceed, regardless of whether it is overall spontaneous. — every reaction, spontaneous or not, must cross this barrier, which is why enzymes (which lower this hump) matter so much biologically. The overall drop in height from start to finish is ΔG.

Endothermic vs exothermic

Exothermic (ΔH negative): releases heat to surroundings — e.g. glucose oxidation during respiration. Endothermic (ΔH positive): absorbs heat from surroundings — e.g. many biosynthesis steps that require ATP energy input to proceed.

🧫 Microbiology Connection — ATP hydrolysis
ATP → ADP + Pi has a strongly negative ΔG (~ −30.5 kJ/mol). Cells "spend" this favorable, energy-releasing reaction to drive their own unfavorable (positive ΔG) reactions, such as active transport or biosynthesis, by coupling the two together.
🧫 Microbiology Connection — Protein folding
A folded protein has lower entropy than an unfolded chain (more ordered), yet folding is still spontaneous overall because it releases enough favorable ΔH (from bond formation and burying hydrophobic residues) to outweigh that entropy cost.

Worked example

A reaction has ΔH = −40 kJ/mol and ΔS = −0.05 kJ/mol·K at T = 310 K (body temperature). Is it spontaneous?

  1. Compute TΔS: 310 × (−0.05) = −15.5 kJ/mol
  2. Apply ΔG = ΔH − TΔS: ΔG = −40 − (−15.5)
  3. Subtracting a negative means adding: ΔG = −40 + 15.5 = −24.5 kJ/mol

Answer: ΔG = −24.5 kJ/mol → negative → spontaneous at body temperature.

Colligative Properties

Properties of a solution that depend only on how many dissolved particles are present — not what those particles chemically are.

Why only particle number matters

Colligative properties (osmotic pressure among them) arise because dissolved particles physically get in the way of solvent molecules — diluting them, slowing their escape, disrupting their organisation. One particle causes one unit of this crowding effect, whether that particle is a sugar molecule, a salt ion, or a protein. So what determines the effect is simply how many separate particles are dissolved, not their chemical identity.

Osmosis and osmotic pressure

OsmosisThe net movement of solvent (water) across a semipermeable membrane, from a region of lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration. is water moving across a membrane toward the side with more dissolved particles, trying to equalise concentration on both sides. Osmotic pressureThe pressure that would need to be applied to stop osmotic water flow. is the pressure you would need to apply to the concentrated side to stop that water movement.

π = M R T
SymbolMeaningUnit
πOsmotic pressureatm
MOsmolarityTotal concentration of all dissolved particles, in osmoles per litre of solution. — total particle concentration (not just molecule concentration; a salt that splits into 2 ions counts as 2)osmol/L
RThe gas constant — a fixed conversion number0.0821 L·atm/mol·K
TAbsolute temperatureKelvin

If particle concentration (M) increases, osmotic pressure rises proportionally — a more concentrated solution pulls water in harder. If temperature rises, osmotic pressure also rises, because warmer particles move and collide more vigorously.

Note: OsmolalitySame idea as osmolarity, but measured per kilogram of solvent rather than per litre of solution — used when precision matters, e.g. in clinical lab reporting. is a closely related term used when measuring per kilogram of solvent instead of per litre of solution — the concept is identical.

Tonicity and the cell

🩺 Clinical Connection — RBC / IV fluids
Normal saline (0.9%) is isotonic with blood, so it is safe to infuse. Pure water infused IV is hypotonic and would cause red blood cells to swell and burst (haemolysis).
🩺 Clinical Connection — Kidney
The kidney's loop of Henle deliberately builds a hypertonic environment in the medulla to draw water out of urine by osmosis, concentrating it.
🧫 Microbiology Connection
Bacteria placed in a strongly hypertonic environment (e.g. high-salt curing, honey) undergo plasmolysisShrinking of the bacterial cytoplasm away from the cell wall due to osmotic water loss. — this is precisely why salting and sugaring are ancient methods of food preservation.

Worked example

Find the osmotic pressure of a 0.10 osmol/L NaCl solution at body temperature (310 K).

  1. Write the formula: π = M R T
  2. Substitute values: π = 0.10 × 0.0821 × 310
  3. Multiply step by step: 0.10 × 0.0821 = 0.00821; then 0.00821 × 310 ≈ 2.55

Answer: π ≈ 2.55 atm — this is the pressure the cell membrane must resist to prevent water rushing in.

Module 01 • Conceptual Foundation

Microscopy: The Big Picture

In microbiological research, microscopy is fundamentally misunderstood when viewed merely as a technique for “making small things look bigger.” magnification alone is trivial; the true physical essence of microscopy is extracting physical contrast to resolve fine structural detail.

Before examining any micrograph, the microbiologist must ask the single most important question in imaging science:

💡
The Fundamental Question of Microscopy:

“What physical signal is the microscope actually detecting to construct this image?”

A microscope image is never an unmediated photograph of reality; it is a spatial map of physical interactions between an incident probe (light photons or fast electrons) and the atoms of the specimen.

🌟

1. Fluorescence Microscopy

Physical Chain:
Excitation Light → fluorophore Absorption → Stokes-Shifted Emission

Primary Strength:
Molecular & cellular target specificity; live-cell compatibility.
🔍

2. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Physical Chain:
Focused Electron Probe → Surface Interaction → Emitted Secondary / Backscattered Electrons

Primary Strength:
Surface morphology, biofilm architecture, and huge depth of field.
⚡

3. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Physical Chain:
Relativistic Electrons → Ultra-thin Specimen → Transmitted / Elastically Diffracted Electrons

Primary Strength:
Internal ultrastructure, viral capsids, cell envelope layers, and atomic lattices.

Module 03 • Light Microscopy & Molecular Labeling

Fluorescence Microscopy: Molecular Specificity

Standard brightfield light microscopy relies on specimens absorbing or refracting transmitted light. Because unstained bacteria are largely transparent (phase objects) with poor intrinsic contrast, traditional microbiology relied on broad chemical dyes (like Gram stains) that kill the organism and offer no molecular specificity.

Fluorescence Microscopy revolutionized biological imaging by converting the specimen itself into an active light emitter. By attaching synthetic fluorescent dyes or genetically encoded fluorescent proteins (such as Green Fluorescent Protein, GFP) to designated cellular targets, scientists can visualize specific proteins, nucleic acids, and organelles against a pitch-black background with exquisite molecular specificity.

Core Principles: How Fluorescence Generates Contrast

Fluorescence occurs in three discrete, sub-nanosecond physical stages:

  1. 1. Excitation: A photon of energy $h\nu_{\text{ex}}$ supplied by an external light source (LED, mercury arc, or laser) is absorbed by the fluorophore, promoting an electron from the ground vibrational singlet state ($S_0$) to an excited electronic singlet state ($S_1$).
  2. 2. Excited-State Lifetime & Internal Relaxation: The fluorophore remains in the excited state for a characteristic fluorescence lifetime ($\tau \approx 1\text{ to }10\text{ nanoseconds}$). During this interval, it dissipates a fraction of its energy through non-radiative molecular vibrations and solvent collisions.
  3. 3. Emission & The Stokes shift: The electron drops back to the ground state ($S_0$), emitting a fluorescence photon ($h\nu_{\text{em}}$). Because vibrational energy was lost during the excited lifetime, the emitted photon has strictly less energy than the absorbed photon ($E_{\text{em}} < E_{\text{ex}}$). According to the Planck-Einstein relation ($E = hc/\lambda$), lower energy equates directly to a longer wavelength:
    $$\lambda_{\text{emission}} > \lambda_{\text{excitation}}$$
    This fundamental wavelength difference is the Stokes shift. It is the physical foundation that makes fluorescence microscopy possible.

The Optical Train: How the Epi-Fluorescence Filter Cube Works

In an epi-fluorescence microscope, the illumination light and emitted fluorescence share the same objective lens. To prevent the intense excitation beam from blinding the observer or camera, a matched optical trio called a Filter Cube (or optical block) is placed in the optical path.

1. excitation filter

Positioned before the dichroic mirror. Transmits only the precise narrow band of wavelengths required to excite the chosen fluorophore, blocking all other lamp wavelengths to prevent background specimen heating and unwanted autofluorescence.

2. dichroic mirror

Tilted at 45°. Coated with thin-film dielectric interference layers. Reflects shorter excitation wavelengths downward through the objective to the sample, while allowing longer emitted fluorescence photons returning through the objective to transmit straight through.

3. barrier (emission) filter

Positioned in front of the camera or eyepiece. Acts as the final quality barrier: transmits only the target fluorescence while blocking any residual reflected excitation light that leaked through the dichroic.

Practical Considerations for Microbiologists

Photobleaching (Fading)

When excited fluorophores react with dissolved oxygen, they form reactive oxygen species (ROS) that permanently destroy the dye molecule's conjugated ring structure. In the laboratory, photobleaching is mitigated by:

  • Minimizing exposure time and utilizing shutter synchronization.
  • Reducing excitation light intensity via neutral density filters.
  • Adding commercial antifade mounting reagents (e.g. ProLong, Vectashield) that scavenge free radicals.

Autofluorescence & Multiple Labeling

Bacterial culture media and intracellular cofactors (flavins, NADH, porphyrins) naturally fluoresce when excited by ultraviolet or blue light. To avoid confusion:

  • Use far-red fluorophores (Cy5, Alexa Fluor 647) where endogenous cellular autofluorescence is near zero.
  • In multi-color staining, choose fluorophores with non-overlapping excitation and emission spectra (e.g. DAPI for DNA + GFP for protein + Texas Red for cell wall).

Specialized Fluorescence Modalities

Beyond standard widefield epifluorescence, several specialized optical configurations extend microbiological analysis:

Near-Field Imaging

TIRF

Excitation light hits the glass coverslip at an angle exceeding the critical angle, creating an evanescent wave that decays exponentially within 100–200 nm. Visualizes bacterial surface motility, flagellar motor complexes, and membrane docking with zero background haze from the cell interior.

Molecular Interactions

FRET

Energy transfers non-radiatively between two fluorophores only when they are within 1 to 10 nm of each other. Serves as a nanometer-scale spectroscopic ruler to detect whether two bacterial virulence proteins are directly bound.

Diffusion & Mobility

FRAP

A focused laser photobleaches a discrete spot on a living cell, and the rate of fluorescence recovery is timed as unbleached molecules diffuse back in. Measures bacterial membrane protein mobility and cytoplasmic viscosity.

Module 04 • Electron Beam & Surface Topography

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM): Surface Morphology

While optical microscopy uses photons of visible light ($\lambda \approx 400 - 700\text{ nm}$), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) harnesses high-energy electrons accelerated to relativistic velocities. Because accelerated electrons have a de Broglie wavelength tens of thousands of times smaller than light ($\lambda < 0.01\text{ nm}$), SEM achieves a spatial resolution of 1 to 5 nanometers with an extraordinary depth of field that gives microbial specimens their dramatic, three-dimensional appearance.

Core Principle: How an SEM Constructs an Image

Unlike light microscopes or TEMs, an SEM contains no optical imaging lenses beneath the specimen. Instead, an image is constructed through raster scanning:

  1. 1. Probe Formation: The electron gun emits electrons that are accelerated across a potential difference (accelerating voltage, 1–30 kV). Condenser lenses demagnify the electron source into an ultra-fine electron probe (1–10 nm in diameter).
  2. 2. Surface Raster Scanning: Electromagnetic deflection coils sweep this focused probe point-by-point across a rectangular grid on the specimen's surface in high vacuum.
  3. 3. Synchronized Point-by-Point Detection: As the probe strikes each $(x,y)$ pixel coordinate on the specimen, electrons and X-rays are emitted. A detector measures the signal intensity at that exact instant. The computer displays a pixel on a monitor whose brightness corresponds directly to the detector's electrical current.

The Three Essential SEM Signals: SE, BSE, and EDS

When fast electrons strike a solid sample, elastic and inelastic scattering creates a tear-drop shaped interaction volume. Different physical signals escape from different depths, giving the microbiologist distinct information:

🏔️

1. Secondary Electrons (SE)

Energy: Low (<50 eV) • Origin: Shallow surface (2–10 nm depth).

Because they escape only from the immediate surface, secondary electron yield depends heavily on surface tilt and edges. Sloped surfaces and fine ridges emit more electrons and appear brightly illuminated, creating exquisite surface topography of bacterial shapes, flagella, and biofilm networks.

⚛️

2. Backscattered Electrons (BSE)

Energy: High ($\approx E_0$) • Origin: Deeper interaction volume (sub-surface).

Primary electrons elastically deflected by atomic nuclei. Scattering cross-section increases with atomic number ($Z$). Heavier elements backscatter far more electrons than organic carbon ($Z=6$), generating compositional contrast (e.g. gold nanoparticles or heavy metal stains shine brightly).

📊

3. Energy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDS / EDX)

Energy: Discrete photon energies (0.1–20 keV) • Origin: Entire interaction volume.

Beam electrons eject core-shell electrons, prompting outer electrons to drop down and emit characteristic X-rays. Measuring these discrete photon energies identifies which chemical elements (e.g. Ca, P, S, Fe, Au) are present in a biofilm or microbial mineral precipitate.

Practical Considerations: Preparing Microbes for the SEM

Dealing with Electrostatic Charging

Biological cells are electrical insulators. Under electron bombardment in high vacuum, negative charge builds up on the surface, deflecting the incident beam and causing blinding white glare and image distortion.

Solution: Specimens are coated with a 2–10 nm layer of gold, platinum, or carbon via conductive sputter coating. The metallic shell grounds the charge and boosts secondary electron emission.

Dehydration and Critical Point Drying (CPD)

Living bacteria are ~70% liquid water, which violently boils and vaporizes inside the SEM vacuum chamber. Air-drying creates immense surface-tension capillary forces that collapse delicate pili, flagella, and EPS matrices.

Solution: Cells are chemically fixed (glutaraldehyde), dehydrated through graded ethanol, and dried via Critical Point Drying (CPD) using liquid $\text{CO}_2$ to bypass liquid-gas phase boundaries completely.

Module 05 • Relativistic Electrons & Internal Ultrastructure

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): Internal Ultrastructure

While SEM visualizes surfaces, Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) is the ultimate optical tool for peering inside microbial cells. By accelerating electrons through massive potentials (80 to 300 kV), electrons attain relativistic velocities (~70% the speed of light) with picometer de Broglie wavelengths ($\lambda \approx 0.00251\text{ nm}$ at 200 kV). These energetic electrons transmit completely through an ultrathin section, resolving bacterial cell walls, peptidoglycan layers, bacteriophages, and atomic crystal lattices with sub-nanometer resolving power.

The Core TEM Pathway: From Source to Dual Conjugate Planes

The optical pathway of a TEM traces an uninterrupted chain from top to bottom:

  1. 1. High-Voltage Acceleration: An electron gun (Schottky or Cold FEG) generates electrons, which are accelerated across 80–300 kV in ultra-high vacuum ($10^{-5}$ to $10^{-8}$ Pa).
  2. 2. Parallel Illumination: Condenser lenses (C1 & C2) form a broad, parallel flood beam that uniformly illuminates the specimen without probe scanning.
  3. 3. Specimen Interaction: Electrons penetrate the ultra-thin foil (<100 nm). Depending on local atomic density, electrons pass unscattered, scatter elastically (Bragg diffraction), or scatter inelastically.
  4. 4. Objective Lens & Dual Conjugate Planes: The objective lens forms two optical planes simultaneously:
    • Back Focal Plane (BFP): Contains the Fourier transform / electron diffraction.
    • First Intermediate Image Plane: Contains the real-space magnified image.
  5. 5. Projection to Detector: Adjusting the intermediate lens focal length toggles which plane is magnified onto the camera screen: focusing on the image plane produces a micrograph; focusing on the BFP displays the diffraction pattern!

Contrast Mechanisms: Bright-Field, Dark-Field, and Phase Contrast

An image in TEM is produced by selectively filtering or interfering electron waves:

☀️

1. Bright-Field (BF) TEM

The objective aperture is centered to admit only the unscattered direct beam (000). Dense or heavy-metal stained biological features scatter electrons outside the aperture, casting dark shadows (mass-thickness contrast). This is the standard mode for biological thin sections.

🌙

2. Dark-Field (DF) TEM

The objective aperture blocks the unscattered beam and selects a specific diffracted Bragg beam. Only crystal grains or nanoparticles tilted at that exact Bragg angle emit electrons through the aperture, causing specific crystallographic phases or defect dislocations to glow brightly against pitch-black.

〰️

3. phase contrast (HRTEM)

For ultra-thin unstained specimens, electrons undergo phase shifts without absorption. The unscattered wave and diffracted waves interfere in the image plane. Controlled underfocus (Scherzer defocus) converts phase variations into atomic-resolution lattice fringes and direct atomic column visualization.

Electron Diffraction: Unlocking Crystal Lattice Structure

When electrons encounter periodic crystalline planes, they undergo coherent constructive interference described by Bragg's Law ($n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$). In Selected-Area Electron Diffraction (SAED), an aperture in the image plane isolates diffraction from a single bacterial inclusion or nanoparticle.

Analytical Extensions: EELS, STEM, and HAADF Z-Contrast

Modern transmission instruments extend beyond simple transmission imaging:

Electron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy (EELS)

A magnetic prism spectrometer bends transmitted electrons according to their residual kinetic energy ($E_0 - \Delta E$). Measures valence plasmons (calculating local foil thickness $t/\lambda = \ln(I_t/I_0)$) and core-loss edges to map light biological elements (C, N, O, P) and chemical oxidation states.

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) & High-Angle Annular Dark-Field (HAADF)

A sub-angstrom electron probe is raster-scanned across the thin specimen. An annular detector collects only electrons scattered to large angles (>50 mrad). At these high angles, scattering is dominated by incoherent Rutherford collisions scaling strictly with atomic number ($\sigma \propto Z^{1.7-2.0}$).

Z-contrast Interpretability

Because HAADF intensity is strictly monotonic with atomic number and mass thickness, there are no phase-contrast reversals with defocus. Heavy atoms appear unmistakably bright, providing unambiguous direct chemical mapping at sub-angstrom resolution!

Why Must Samples Be Ultra-Thin? Specimen Preparation & Cryo-EM

⚠️
The Physical Thickness Constraint of TEM (<100 nm):

Electrons interact strongly with matter. In a specimen thicker than 100 nm, fast electrons suffer repeated inelastic plasmon collisions. The resulting energy loss creates severe chromatic aberration blur ($d_c = C_c \frac{\Delta E}{E_0}\alpha$) and total beam absorption. For high resolution, biological specimens must be thinned to 10 to 50 nanometers.

1. Ultramicrotomy & heavy metal staining

Bacterial pellets or tissues are fixed (glutaraldehyde + $\text{OsO}_4$), dehydrated in ethanol, and embedded in hard plastic epoxy. An ultramicrotome equipped with a 35°/45° diamond knife cuts ribbons 50 to 70 nm thick that float on water onto copper grids.

Because organic atoms ($H, C, N, O$) scatter poorly, sections are stained with heavy metal salts: Uranyl Acetate ($U, Z=92$) binds nucleic acids and proteins; Lead Citrate ($Pb, Z=82$) stains lipid membranes.

2. cryofixation (vitrification) & Single Particle Analysis

Heavy metal staining and plastic dehydration introduce structural artifacts. Cryo-EM (awarded the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry) preserves proteins, viruses, and microbes in their native, fully hydrated functional state.

Specimens are rapidly plunge-frozen into liquid ethane cooled to $-183^\circ\text{C}$ (cooling rate $>10^5\text{ K/s}$). Liquid water solidifies into amorphous vitreous ice without expanding into destructive crystalline lattices, allowing near-atomic 3D reconstructions.

Module 07 • Image Interpretation Theory

“What Does the Image Actually Mean?” The Physics of Pixel Brightness

A frequent mistake in student microscopy is treating micrographs as ordinary photographs where bright areas represent "white objects" and dark areas represent "shadows." In truth, pixel brightness is a quantitative measurement of detector signal response governed by distinct physical laws in each technique.

Fluorescence: Molecular Emitter Density

What Brightness Means:
A bright pixel indicates that a high flux of Stokes-shifted emission photons struck that pixel during the camera exposure. It represents the local spatial concentration of active fluorophores.

Student Trap: A dark area does not prove an organelle is missing; it only proves that the fluorophore did not bind, was washed away, or was photobleached!

SEM: Surface Slope & Atomic Number ($Z$)

What Brightness Means:
In Secondary Electron (SE) mode, brightness represents surface slope: edges and tilted planes allow more low-energy electrons to escape, shining brightly. In Backscatter (BSE) mode, brightness represents mean atomic number ($Z$).

Student Trap: The apparent "illumination" from the upper-left is an optical illusion created by the detector's physical position in the vacuum chamber, not real sunlight!

TEM: Mass-Thickness & Phase Shifts

What Brightness Means:
In Bright-Field TEM, darkness represents electrons removed from the beam by high-angle Rutherford scattering or Bragg diffraction outside the objective aperture. In HRTEM, brightness/darkness represents wave interference phase shifts.

Student Trap: A TEM image is a 2D projection of a 50 nm 3D slab; structures at the top, middle, and bottom of the foil are compressed on top of one another!
Module 08 • Practical Microbiology Lab

Microbiology Applications: Bench Examples

How do these three microscopes address real-world challenges in bacteriology, virology, and biofilm research? Explore the concrete applications below:

🧫

Fluorescence in Microbiology

  • Live/Dead Bacterial Viability Assays: Dual staining using membrane-permeable SYTO 9 (stains all cells green) and membrane-impermeable Propidium Iodide (penetrates only damaged membranes, staining dead cells red).
  • Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH): Fluorophore-conjugated oligonucleotide probes targeting 16S rRNA to identify unculturable pathogen species in clinical sputum or soil samples.
  • Protein Localization (GFP / mCherry): Tracking FtsZ division-ring assembly or MreB actin cytoskeleton filaments in dividing E. coli cells.
  • Biofilm Matrix Architecture: Fluorescent lectins (e.g. Concanavalin A-FITC) binding specifically to extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) matrices.
🔬

SEM in Microbiology

  • Bacterial Morphology & Pleomorphism: Distinguishing rod-shaped bacilli, spherical staphylococci clusters, and helical spirochetes during antibiotic treatment.
  • Cell Surface Appendages: Visualizing peritrichous flagella, sex pili, and adhesion fimbriae mediating attachment to host epithelial tissues.
  • Biofilm Architecture on Medical Implants: Characterizing three-dimensional bacterial biofilms, slime networks, and water channels on prosthetic joints and catheters.
  • Fungal Hyphae & Conidia: High-magnification surface visualization of spore release structures in Aspergillus and Penicillium.
🧬

TEM in Microbiology

  • Gram-Positive vs Gram-Negative Envelopes: High-resolution cross-sections distinguishing thick (20–80 nm) peptidoglycan walls from thin (2–7 nm) peptidoglycan flanked by an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane.
  • Bacteriophage & Viral Anatomy: Negative staining (Uranyl Acetate) revealing the icosahedral head, contractile neck, baseplate, and tail fibers of T4 phages.
  • Intracellular Inclusion Bodies: Visualizing bacterial storage granules: polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), polyphosphate volutin granules, and magnetosomes.
  • Endospore Ultrastructure: Cross-sectional anatomy of bacterial endospores (e.g. Bacillus anthracis): core DNA, cortex, spore coat, and exosporium layers.

Module 10 • Critical Evaluation

Microscopy Artifacts & Operational Limitations

A competent scientist never accepts a micrograph at face value without interrogating the potential for preparation artifacts.

⚖️
The Central Axiom of Microscopy:

“Every microscopy image is a measurement produced by an imaging system, not an unfiltered view of reality.”

Fluorescence Pitfalls

  • Photobleaching: Signal dims continuously during observation; quantitative intensity measurements require exposure control.
  • Autofluorescence: Media and endogenous NADH emit background haze, obscuring weak specific probe signals.
  • Spectral Bleed-Through: Overlapping emission spectra in multi-color assays create false co-localization artifacts.
  • Resolution Limit: Standard widefield cannot resolve structures closer than ~200 nm regardless of magnification.

SEM Pitfalls

  • Electrostatic Charging: Non-conductive dry bacteria build up charge, causing bright blinding glare and image tearing.
  • Dehydration Collapse: Inadequate critical-point drying causes capillary collapse of delicate flagella and EPS matrices.
  • Edge Blooming: Secondary electrons escape readily from sharp peaks, making fine tips look artificially bright.
  • Surface-Only Limitation: Cannot visualize intracellular cytoplasm, organelles, or internal membranes.

TEM Pitfalls

  • Ultramicrotomy Chatter: Diamond knife vibration introduces periodic thickness variations across the 50 nm foil.
  • Stain Precipitation: Uranyl acetate or lead citrate crystals precipitate, deceptively mimicking viral particles.
  • Radiation Beam Damage: High-kV electrons break covalent bonds (radiolysis) and displace atoms (knock-on damage).
  • 2D Projection Overlap: Features at different depths along the beam trajectory overlap on top of one another.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) SEM
An electron microscopy technique where a finely focused electron probe is raster-scanned across a specimen surface, collecting emitted secondary electrons (SE), backscattered electrons (BSE), and characteristic X-rays pixel by pixel.
Why it matters: Provides large depth-of-field, three-dimensional views of bacterial surface morphology, biofilm architecture, cell division septa, and flagella.
Related: Raster ScanningSecondary ElectronsBackscattered ElectronsEnergy-Dispersive X-Ray SpectroscopyDepth of Field
Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy STEM
An electron microscopy hybrid that focuses the electron beam into an ultra-fine, sub-nanometer convergent probe and rasters it across an electron-transparent foil, collecting transmitted electrons on annular detectors.
Why it matters: Enables simultaneous HAADF Z-contrast imaging, atomic-resolution EDS elemental mapping, and 4D-STEM diffraction per pixel.
Related: Transmission Electron MicroscopyScanning Electron MicroscopyHigh-Angle Annular Dark-FieldRaster Scanning
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) TEM
An electron microscopy modality where a broad, parallel beam of high-energy relativistic electrons (80–300 keV) transmits through an ultrathin specimen (<100 nm), forming 2D projection images and electron diffraction patterns at sub-nanometer to atomic resolution.
Why it matters: The definitive technique for resolving internal bacterial ultrastructure, cell envelope layers (Gram-positive peptidoglycan vs. Gram-negative double membrane), inclusion bodies, and viral/bacteriophage morphology.
Related: Bright-FieldDark-FieldElectron DiffractionHigh-Resolution Transmission Electron MicroscopyCryofixation / VitrificationUltrathin Section
Vacuum System High / Ultra-High Vacuum
Evacuated column and chamber ($10^{-3}$ to $10^{-8}$ Pa) maintained by roughing, turbomolecular, and ion-getter pumps to eliminate atmospheric gas molecules.
Why it matters: Without high vacuum, electron beams would collide with gas molecules, scatter, and be absorbed within millimeters. Requires specimens to be completely dry or vitrified.
Related: Scanning Electron MicroscopyTransmission Electron MicroscopyCryofixation / Vitrification

Signals, Contrast & Detection

Backscattered Electrons (BSE) BSE / BSD
High-energy primary beam electrons that undergo large-angle elastic Rutherford collisions with specimen atomic nuclei and re-emerge from the sample surface with minimal energy loss.
Why it matters: Yield scales strongly with atomic number ($Z$), providing compositional contrast: heavier elements (e.g. gold immunolabels, heavy stains) appear significantly brighter than organic carbon.
Related: Scanning Electron MicroscopySecondary ElectronsElastic ScatteringAtomic-Number Contrast
Bremsstrahlung (Braking Radiation) Continuum Background
Continuous background X-ray radiation generated when energetic beam electrons are decelerated in the Coulomb field of specimen atomic nuclei, losing energy as continuous photons.
Why it matters: Forms the continuous background in EDS spectra that must be subtracted during quantitative elemental analysis.
Related: Characteristic X-RaysEnergy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy
Bright-Field (BF) TEM BF-TEM
The conventional TEM imaging mode where an objective aperture selects only the unscattered direct electron beam. Dense, thick, or heavy-atom stained regions scatter electrons outside the aperture and appear dark against a bright background.
Why it matters: The primary workhorse mode in biological TEM for examining ultrastructural anatomy of stained bacterial and cellular thin sections.
Related: Dark-FieldMass-Thickness ContrastTransmission Electron MicroscopyObjective Lens
Characteristic X-Rays X-Ray Emission
Discrete photon emissions produced when an incoming fast electron knocks out an inner-shell core electron, and an outer-shell electron drops down to fill the vacancy, emitting energy matching the atomic shell difference.
Why it matters: The emission energies serve as unique atomic fingerprints for chemical identification in EDS/EDX microanalysis.
Related: Energy-Dispersive X-Ray SpectroscopyInelastic ScatteringScanning Electron MicroscopyTransmission Electron Microscopy
Dark-Field (DF) TEM DF-TEM
An imaging mode where the direct central beam is blocked by the objective aperture, and only electrons scattered or diffracted at specific angles are collected to form the image.
Why it matters: Crystalline structures, defect lines, nanoparticles, and specific orientations glow brightly against an otherwise dark background.
Related: Bright-FieldElectron DiffractionTransmission Electron Microscopy
High-Angle Annular Dark-Field (HAADF) STEM HAADF-STEM
An annular detector setup in STEM that intercepts only electrons scattered to large angles (>50 mrad), where coherent diffraction is suppressed and incoherent Rutherford nuclear scattering dominates ($\sigma \propto Z^{1.7-2.0}$).
Why it matters: Produces monotonic atomic-number ($Z$) contrast without phase-contrast reversals; heavy atoms glow brightly against light-atom backgrounds.
Related: Scanning Transmission Electron MicroscopyAtomic-Number ContrastTransmission Electron MicroscopyBackscattered Electrons
Phase Contrast (Wave Interference) HRTEM Contrast
Contrast generated by the quantum mechanical phase shift experienced by electron waves traversing variations in a specimen's electrostatic potential, converted into intensity variations by lens aberrations and defocus.
Why it matters: The primary contrast mechanism for unstained cryo-EM specimens and atomic-resolution lattice imaging (HRTEM).
Related: High-Resolution Transmission Electron MicroscopyContrast Transfer FunctionTransmission Electron Microscopy
Secondary Electrons (SE) SE
Low-energy electrons (<50 eV) ejected from specimen valence orbitals by inelastic collisions with incoming primary beam electrons. Due to their low energy, only those generated within 2–10 nm of the surface escape.
Why it matters: Provides high-resolution topographical imaging of bacterial surfaces, biofilm meshes, and cell appendages.
Related: Scanning Electron MicroscopyBackscattered ElectronsRaster Scanning

Crystallography & Advanced Physics

Bragg's Law nλ = 2d sin θ
The fundamental mathematical relationship governing coherent wave diffraction from periodic crystal planes: $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$, where $d$ is interplanar spacing, $\lambda$ is wavelength, and $\theta$ is the Bragg angle.
Why it matters: Allows calculation of sub-nanometer atomic plane spacing directly from electron diffraction spot measurements.
Related: Electron DiffractionSelected-Area Electron DiffractionTransmission Electron Microscopy
Electron Diffraction Bragg Diffraction
The constructive and destructive wave interference of elastically scattered electrons passing through regular planes of atoms in a crystal, satisfying Bragg's Law ($n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta_B$).
Why it matters: Produces spot or ring patterns in the objective back focal plane that reveal crystal symmetry, lattice constants ($d_{hkl}$), and phase identity.
Related: Selected-Area Electron DiffractionBragg's LawTransmission Electron Microscopy
Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) FRAP
An experimental protocol where a high-intensity laser pulse intentionally photobleaches a discrete spot on a fluorescently tagged cell, followed by monitoring the rate at which unbleached molecules diffuse back into that region.
Why it matters: Measures molecular mobility, membrane fluidity, and diffusion coefficients of bacterial proteins in vivo.
Related: PhotobleachingFluorophore
Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) FRET
Non-radiative dipole-dipole energy transfer from an excited donor fluorophore to an acceptor fluorophore when they are located within 1 to 10 nanometers of each other.
Why it matters: Acts as a 'spectroscopic ruler' to detect nanometer-scale protein-protein interactions and conformational changes inside living bacteria.
Related: FluorophoreStokes ShiftQuenching
High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy HRTEM
An advanced phase-contrast TEM imaging mode that uses multi-beam interference between the unscattered beam and diffracted electron waves to resolve crystalline lattice fringes and individual atomic columns.
Why it matters: Visualizes crystal defects, nanoparticle facet arrangements, and atomic interfaces with sub-angstrom precision.
Related: Phase ContrastContrast Transfer FunctionScherzer DefocusTransmission Electron Microscopy
Selected-Area Electron Diffraction (SAED / SAD) SAED / SAD
A TEM diffraction technique where a mechanical aperture is inserted into the first intermediate image plane to isolate diffraction patterns from a defined sub-micrometer specimen area ($0.1 - 2\ \mu\text{m}$).
Why it matters: Enables crystallographic characterization of individual bacterial mineral inclusions (e.g. magnetosomes) without interference from surrounding matrix.
Related: Electron DiffractionBragg's LawTransmission Electron Microscopy
Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence (TIRF) TIRF
An advanced optical technique that directs excitation light at an angle beyond the critical angle at a glass-water interface, generating a shallow evanescent wave that penetrates only 100–200 nm into the specimen.
Why it matters: Eliminates out-of-focus background, allowing microbiologists to visualize individual cell-membrane receptors and bacterial adhesion processes.
Related: FluorescenceNumerical ApertureSpatial Resolving Power

Sample Preparation & Microbiology

Autofluorescence
Natural endogenous fluorescence emitted by biological components (such as NADH, flavins, collagen, chlorophyll, or glutaraldehyde fixatives) in the absence of added fluorescent dyes.
Why it matters: Creates unwanted background haze in bacterial samples; can be mitigated by choosing far-red fluorophores.
Related: FluorophoreContrastSignal-to-Noise Ratio
Charging Artifact
The buildup of negative electrostatic charge on the surface of non-conductive specimens (such as biological cells, polymers, and ceramics) when irradiated by an electron beam in vacuum.
Why it matters: Causes bright glare, image distortion, and beam deflection; prevented by sputter coating with a thin conductive film (Au/Pt/C) or using low-vacuum modes.
Related: Scanning Electron MicroscopyVacuum SystemSecondary Electrons
Conductive Sputter Coating Au/Pt/C Coating
The vacuum deposition of an ultra-thin (2–10 nm) layer of a noble metal (gold, platinum) or carbon onto a dehydrated non-conductive specimen.
Why it matters: Provides a path to ground electrical charge and increases secondary electron emission for crisp SEM imaging of microbes.
Related: Charging ArtifactScanning Electron MicroscopyVacuum System
Cryofixation / Vitrification Rapid Plunge-Freezing
Ultra-rapid freezing of biological samples by plunging them into liquid ethane cooled to $-183^\circ\text{C}$ (cooling rate $>10^5\text{ K/s}$), freezing liquid water so fast that crystal ice cannot nucleate, forming amorphous vitreous ice.
Why it matters: Preserves macromolecules and microbes in their native, fully hydrated functional state without plastic embedding, chemical fixation, or heavy-metal stain artifacts (the foundation of Cryo-EM).
Related: VitreousUltrastructureTransmission Electron Microscopy
Heavy Metal Contrast Staining UA / Lead Staining
The application of salts of heavy elements—predominantly Uranyl Acetate ($U, Z=92$) and Lead Citrate ($Pb, Z=82$)—which bind selectively to nucleic acids, proteins, and lipid membranes.
Why it matters: Unstained biological macromolecules consist of light elements ($H, C, N, O$) with poor scattering power; heavy metal stains impart strong mass-thickness contrast.
Related: Ultrathin SectionMass-Thickness ContrastBright-Field
Photobleaching Fading
The permanent photochemical destruction of a fluorophore's ability to fluoresce, caused by repeated light absorption and interactions with reactive oxygen species while in the excited state.
Why it matters: Requires microbiologists to minimize exposure times, lower illumination power, and add antifade mounting reagents.
Related: QuenchingFluorophoreFluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching
Quenching
Non-radiative dissipation of excitation energy from a fluorophore due to collisions or complex formation with other molecules (e.g. molecular oxygen, halides, heavy metals, or high local fluorophore concentration).
Why it matters: Reduces fluorescence intensity without chemically destroying the fluorophore; must be controlled during live-cell assays.
Related: PhotobleachingFluorophoreFörster Resonance Energy Transfer
Ultrastructure Fine Structure
The detailed internal structural organization of a biological cell, tissue, or virus that is beyond the resolving power of a conventional optical light microscope (i.e. finer than 200 nm).
Why it matters: Includes bacterial cell walls, peptidoglycan layers, ribosomes, nuclear bodies, flagellar basal bodies, and viral capsid envelopes.
Related: Ultrathin SectionTransmission Electron MicroscopyCryofixation / Vitrification
Ultrathin Section (Ultramicrotomy) 50–70 nm Foil
A biological specimen embedded in hard epoxy or acrylic plastic resin and sliced to a thickness of 50 to 70 nanometers using an ultramicrotome equipped with a precision diamond knife.
Why it matters: Mandatory for biological TEM so high-energy electrons can transmit through the sample without multiple inelastic scattering blur.
Related: UltrastructureHeavy Metal Contrast StainingTransmission Electron Microscopy
Vitreous (Amorphous) Ice Glassy Water
A disordered, non-crystalline solid state of water formed when liquid is cooled with extreme speed, lacking the hexagonal crystal lattice that would otherwise expand and rupture cell membranes.
Why it matters: Provides an electron-transparent, protective matrix for imaging proteins and viruses at cryogenic temperatures.
Related: Cryofixation / VitrificationTransmission Electron Microscopy

Microscopy Fundamentals

Atomic-Number Contrast (Z-Contrast) Z-Contrast
Image contrast where signal intensity scales monotonically with the atomic number ($Z$) of the specimen atoms due to Rutherford scattering cross-sections.
Why it matters: Allows immediate chemical differentiation between heavy elements and light organic matrices without complex phase simulation.
Related: High-Angle Annular Dark-FieldBackscattered ElectronsScanning Transmission Electron Microscopy
Barrier Filter (Emission Filter) EM Filter
An optical filter positioned between the dichroic mirror and detector that transmits only the target emitted fluorescence while completely blocking any residual reflected excitation light.
Why it matters: Ensures the background in fluorescence images remains pitch-black so faint labeled cellular features stand out with high contrast.
Related: Dichroic MirrorExcitation FilterEmission
Contrast C = ΔI / I
The relative difference in luminance, color, or electron intensity that makes an object distinguishable from other objects and the background: $C = (I_{\text{feature}} - I_{\text{background}}) / I_{\text{background}}$.
Why it matters: Without contrast, an object can be resolved by optical diffraction yet remain completely invisible to the eye or camera.
Related: Signal-to-Noise RatioMass-Thickness ContrastPhase Contrast
Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) CTF
A mathematical sinusoidal function ($T(u) = -2\sin(\chi(u))$) describing how spatial frequencies (structural details) are transferred from the specimen to the image as a function of defocus and spherical aberration.
Why it matters: Understanding the CTF prevents misinterpreting contrast inversions (where atom columns flip between bright and dark dots) and is essential for Cryo-EM 3D reconstructions.
Related: Phase ContrastHigh-Resolution Transmission Electron MicroscopyScherzer Defocus
Depth of Field DoF
The axial distance along the optical axis over which a specimen remains in acceptable sharp focus simultaneously without refocusing.
Why it matters: SEM has a depth of field hundreds of times greater than light microscopy, creating stunning 3D-like images of rough biological surfaces and biofilms.
Related: Scanning Electron MicroscopySpatial Resolving PowerMagnification
Dichroic Mirror (Beam Splitter) Dichromatic Beamsplitter
A specialized interference filter tilted at 45° that reflects shorter excitation wavelengths down toward the specimen while transmitting longer fluorescence emission wavelengths through to the detector.
Why it matters: The core optical gatekeeper in an epi-fluorescence microscope, isolating emission signals from excitation glare.
Related: Excitation FilterBarrier FilterStokes Shift
Elastic Scattering ΔE = 0
Electron-matter collisions where beam electrons are deflected by nuclear Coulomb forces without significant kinetic energy loss ($\Delta E = 0$), conserving momentum and wavelength.
Why it matters: Generates the phase shifts and Bragg diffracted beams that produce crystallographic diffraction patterns and atomic phase contrast.
Related: Inelastic ScatteringElectron DiffractionTransmission Electron Microscopy
Electron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy (EELS) EELS
An analytical technique that measures the residual kinetic energy of forward-transmitted electrons using a magnetic prism spectrometer to determine the energy lost to plasmons and core-shell ionizations.
Why it matters: Far superior to EDS for light elements ($Z < 11$: C, N, O, P) and capable of determining local specimen thickness and chemical oxidation states.
Related: Energy-Dispersive X-Ray SpectroscopyInelastic ScatteringTransmission Electron MicroscopyScanning Transmission Electron Microscopy
Energy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDS / EDX) EDS / EDX
An analytical microanalysis technique that measures the energy distribution of characteristic X-rays emitted during electron bombardment to identify and quantify chemical elements present in the specimen.
Why it matters: Allows microbiologists to map elemental compositions (e.g. sulfur, phosphorus, mineral precipitates, metal nanoparticles) across microbial samples.
Related: Characteristic X-RaysScanning Electron MicroscopyTransmission Electron MicroscopyElectron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy
Excitation Filter EX Filter
A bandpass optical filter placed between the light source and dichroic mirror that selects only the precise narrow wavelength band needed to excite the target fluorophore.
Why it matters: Prevents extraneous light wavelengths from reaching the sample and causing unwanted background autofluorescence.
Related: Dichroic MirrorBarrier FilterExcitation
Inelastic Scattering ΔE > 0
Electron-matter interactions where beam electrons transfer discrete packets of kinetic energy to specimen atomic electrons (causing plasmons, ionization, and characteristic emissions).
Why it matters: Generates analytical signals (EDS X-rays, EELS energy loss, secondary electrons) but also causes specimen heating, radiation damage, and chromatic blur.
Related: Elastic ScatteringCharacteristic X-RaysElectron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy
Magnification M (×)
The numerical ratio of the physical size of an image to the actual dimensions of the object: $M = \text{Image Size} / \text{Object Size}$.
Why it matters: Magnification without adequate resolving power produces 'empty magnification'—a larger image that reveals no additional structural detail.
Related: Spatial Resolving PowerDepth of Field
Mass-Thickness Contrast Incoherent Contrast
Image contrast arising from variations in specimen density ($\rho$), thickness ($t$), and atomic number ($Z$). Regions with higher mass-thickness scatter electrons through larger angles via Rutherford collisions.
Why it matters: Explains why biological membranes stained with heavy metals (osmium, lead, uranium) appear sharply dark in bright-field TEM.
Related: Bright-FieldElastic ScatteringHeavy Metal Contrast Staining
Numerical Aperture (NA) NA
A dimensionless number characterizing the range of angles over which an optical system can accept or emit light: $\text{NA} = n \sin \alpha$, where $n$ is refractive index and $\alpha$ is the semi-angle.
Why it matters: Determines resolving power ($\delta \propto 1/\text{NA}$) and brightness ($I \propto \text{NA}^4$ in epi-fluorescence); higher NA captures more emitted photons.
Related: Spatial Resolving PowerRayleigh Resolution CriterionObjective Lens
Objective Lens Objective
The primary imaging lens positioned closest to the specimen. It forms the initial magnified image and simultaneously creates the Back Focal Plane containing the Fourier transform (diffraction pattern).
Why it matters: The ultimate resolving power and aberration performance of both light and transmission electron microscopes are governed predominantly by the objective lens.
Related: Electromagnetic LensNumerical ApertureSpatial Resolving Power
Rayleigh Resolution Criterion δ = 0.61λ / NA
The optical condition establishing that two point sources are just resolved when the central maximum of the Airy diffraction disc of one coincides with the first minimum of the other: $\delta = 0.61\lambda / \text{NA}$.
Why it matters: Explains why optical microscopy is fundamentally diffraction-limited to ~200 nm, and why electron microscopy achieves sub-nanometer resolving power.
Related: Spatial Resolving PowerNumerical Aperturede Broglie Electron Wavelength
Scherzer Defocus Δf_Sch
The specific objective underfocus ($\Delta f_{\text{Sch}} = -1.2\sqrt{C_s\lambda}$) that optimally balances positive spherical aberration, producing a broad, flat passband of constant negative phase contrast.
Why it matters: Ensures that atomic columns in High-Resolution TEM appear with maximum dark contrast directly matching atomic coordinates.
Related: Contrast Transfer FunctionHigh-Resolution Transmission Electron MicroscopyPhase Contrast
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) SNR
The ratio of true structural signal intensity to unwanted random background fluctuations (thermal noise, readout noise, Poisson shot noise).
Why it matters: High resolution is useless if SNR is too low to distinguish real cellular structures from background statistical noise.
Related: ContrastSpatial Resolving PowerAutofluorescence
Spatial Resolving Power (Resolution) d / r
The minimum physical separation distance at which two closely spaced object points can be clearly distinguished as distinct entities rather than a single blurred spot.
Why it matters: Governed by Abbe's and Rayleigh's wave criteria; resolution reveals new physical information, whereas magnification merely enlarges existing detail.
Related: MagnificationRayleigh Resolution CriterionNumerical Aperture
de Broglie Electron Wavelength λ
The quantum wave-packet wavelength of an accelerated electron: $\lambda = h / p \approx 1.226 / \sqrt{V}\text{ nm}$ (with relativistic corrections). At 200 kV, $\lambda = 0.00251\text{ nm}$ (2.51 picometers).
Why it matters: Because electron wavelengths are ~100,000× smaller than visible light photons, electron microscopes achieve spatial resolution down to atomic scales.
Related: Accelerating VoltageSpatial Resolving PowerRayleigh Resolution Criterion
